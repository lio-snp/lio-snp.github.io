0. Two curves that seem to disagree

I took the same model checkpoints and the same texts, and computed two numbers:

  • Cosine similarity between the final-layer representations and step 0, the randomly initialized model: −0.003 near the end of training, or almost orthogonal.
  • Cosine similarity between the final-layer representations and the preceding checkpoint: a median of +0.993, with only one interval below 0.9 across training.

On the same data, one seems to say “everything has changed,” while the other says “hardly anything moved.” Both numbers can be right. What I had missed was that “How far has the model drifted?” and “Has it stopped changing?” depend on both the reference point and the metric. I hadn't kept those two choices separate.

TL;DR

  1. I started out hoping that one curve would tell me whether the model had stopped changing. Then I realized I had changed two things at once.
  2. I thought the small late-training changes might just reflect a learning rate that hadn't reached zero. Looking closer, drift speed was correlated with the learning rate, but an approximately 22-fold jump near 116k needed a separate look (Section 4.3).
  3. Along the way, I found problems in the metric implementation and checkpoint labels. Section 7 records that debugging process.

1. Two starting points

Two kinds of observations about language-model representations got me thinking:

  • Mechanisms: interpretable structures can appear during training. One example is the induction head, an attention circuit that helps copy patterns from earlier context. Its emergence is associated with changes in in-context learning.
  • Evaluation: an ability can look as though it appears suddenly because of how it is scored. A continuous metric can turn an abrupt-looking change into a smoother curve. Some apparent emergence may come from the measurement.

These are different questions: one concerns mechanisms and abilities; the other concerns metric effects in evaluations across model scales. For me, they pointed toward a shared measurement question: in training dynamics, whether a change looks abrupt or gradual depends a lot on how we measure it.

That led me to something more specific: across Pythia's 154 checkpoints, do representations mostly drift continuously, or do they sometimes jump?

Four terms before we start

  • Checkpoint: a saved copy of the model at a particular training step. Pythia releases 154 of them, from step 0 (random initialization) to step 143000. On Hugging Face, these are available as revisions.
  • Activation: a number describing how strongly a neuron responds to a piece of text.
  • Hidden state: an internal vector of 2048 numbers in this model. It isn't something I can read directly, so a projection can help me visualize it as a point on a plane.
  • Orthogonal: two vectors at a 90° angle have a cosine similarity of 0. One detail matters here: in 2048 dimensions, random vectors already tend to have cosine similarity near 0. So “cosine ≈ 0” doesn't automatically mean a dramatic transformation. It means the directions aren't particularly aligned.

2. Setup

I used Pythia-1B because its 154 intermediate checkpoints come from the same training run, with a consistent recipe and data.

I wrote a probe set of 126 texts: 9 categories (punctuation, numbers/dates, syntax, mathematics, code, few-shot ICL, names, sentiment, and definitions) × Chinese and English × 7 examples per combination. I wanted a mix because I was interested in what activates the same neuron at different stages, which calls for more than one kind of input.

At each checkpoint, I ran a forward pass and recorded mean-pooled and last-token hidden states at 5 sampled layers (0/4/8/12/15), peak activations for each MLP neuron and the tokens that triggered them, and loss on the probe texts.

Processing the 154 checkpoints took about 4.7 hours on two GPUs. For each checkpoint, I downloaded the revision's weights (4 GB), ran the forward pass, saved the derived statistics, and deleted the temporary weight files.

3. Loss and representation change

Start with the bottom-right panel: loss on these 126 texts fell from 10.98 → 3.40. For context, with Pythia's vocabulary of 50304 tokens, a uniform guess gives a loss of 10.83. By the end, the model was doing much better than that baseline.

It wasn't a smooth descent, though. Loss rose in 56 of the 153 adjacent intervals, with the largest increase at step 90000 (+0.55). The overall trend was clear; individual intervals were noisy.

In the top-left panel, the final layer's cosine similarity to step 0 fell from 1.00 to −0.003, close to orthogonal.

Takeaway: loss generally decreased on these texts, while representations moved away from their initial directions. The loss curve alone doesn't tell me whether the representations stopped changing.

4. Three metrics, three questions

I had hoped one curve would answer “Has it stopped?” Instead, I found myself asking three different questions about distance from initialization, displacement between checkpoints, and relationships among samples.

4.1 Distance from initialization: how far from the starting point?

In the layer-wise profiles at the bottom left, every layer starts at 1.0 and drops sharply over the first few dozen steps.

Here are a few values for layer 15:

The sharpest change here is from step 8 to 16 (0.906 → 0.300), rather than at step 8. This interval also falls within warmup: Pythia's learning rate rises linearly from 0 to 2.5e-4 over 1430 steps. That reminded me to consider the increasing learning rate when interpreting the speed of these early changes.

A point I initially got wrong: I first described this as “82.8% of the drift completed by step 100.” There were two problems. The actual checkpoint was step 128: my script selected the nearest checkpoint but printed the requested step. More fundamentally, the quantity isn't monotonic. It was 87.9% at step 32 and fell to 82.3% at step 64. Calling it “completion” gives the wrong impression.

Takeaway: distance from initialization describes how far the model is from its starting point. It doesn't measure how much change has accumulated recently.

4.2 Adjacent displacement: how much changed in this interval?

Next I looked at displacement between neighboring checkpoints, divided by the number of training steps because later checkpoints are spaced farther apart. For layer 15:

Movement back and forth can cancel out, so comparing early 8-step intervals with late 1000-step intervals still has limitations. (ii) Both rows above use cosine similarity. My first comparison used cosine in one case and L2 in the other, changing two things at once.

4.3 The learning-rate trend and a jump

I then considered another possibility: the small late-training displacement might reflect a learning rate that was still nonzero. I reconstructed Pythia's published schedule: a peak of 2.5e-4, cosine decay to 2.5e-5, and 1430 warmup steps.

QuantityResult
Learning-rate decrease10-fold (2.5e-4 → 2.5e-5)
Drift-speed decrease~2.5 orders of magnitude
Post-warmup slope of log(speed) vs log(learning rate)0.71 – 0.89
Fit R²0.58 – 0.76

The fit has an R² of 0.58–0.76. Over the fitted interval, log(speed) and log(learning rate) are correlated. That alone doesn't establish the learning rate as the cause, and the fit doesn't capture every fluctuation.

So I wouldn't yet interpret the nonzero late-training displacement as change independent of the learning rate. Looking at the residuals, I noticed an unusual interval near 116k:

Between step 115k and 116k, layer 15's displacement per step rose from 0.0783 to 1.7007, about 22-fold, while the learning rate was decreasing smoothly. Two consecutive intervals (115k→116k and 116k→117k) had values of this order. The median in the tail (≥133k) was 0.0756; the event was 11.8 times that value.

I ran a few consistency checks. The five weight files for step114000–118000 on Hugging Face have different sha256 hashes. This shows that their contents differ, but it doesn't rule out loading or numerical-precision issues. In the representations, I also observed:

  • Coherent displacement directions: the 126 probe texts moved in nearly the same direction. In the middle layers, cos(individual displacement, mean displacement) = +0.99, compared with 0.58 in an ordinary interval (133k→134k).
  • Adjacent-checkpoint cosine similarity dropped from 0.998 → 0.184 and then rebounded (panel (c)). Last-token representations changed too (0.997 → 0.339), with changes across the sampled layers.
  • After subtracting the respective means and projecting out the common direction, cosine similarity was still only around 0.6. A difference remained, but this result alone doesn't establish that it was a rotation.

Mean loss changed relatively little on the current probe set (+0.013 nats). Category-level changes went both ways (code −0.053, names +0.117). That doesn't establish that the model's other behaviors were unaffected.

For now, I would separate the observations into two parts:

  1. Drift speed is correlated with the learning rate, with an R² of 0.58–0.76 in the fit above.
  2. There is an approximately 22-fold jump in representation displacement near 116k. Across these probe texts, displacement directions are fairly consistent, without a comparably striking change in mean loss. I don't yet know what caused it.

I originally treated this interval as an ordinary fluctuation under a changing learning rate. It seems more useful now to keep it separate and investigate further.

5. CKA and cosine ask different questions

CKA (Centered Kernel Alignment) compares the similarity structure among samples in two sets of representations. In the 154×154 heatmap at the top left:

  • Step 0 has dark comparisons with the trained checkpoints. Initialization stands apart under this metric.
  • Comparisons among late checkpoints are mostly bright (CKA ≈ 1). On this probe set, their sample relationships look similar under CKA.
  • For the middle layer (layer 8), CKA relative to step 0 decreases from 1.0 to 0.134. The final layer ends at 0.321.

Put alongside cosine, this takes a little care to read: the final layer's cosine similarity to step 0 is ≈ 0, but its CKA is still 0.32.

It's tempting to call this “just a rotation, with sample relationships preserved.” That doesn't follow. If two representation matrices differ only by an orthogonal transformation, linear CKA should be 1, rather than 0.32. To interpret the intermediate value, I needed a comparison.

I added a shuffled control: break the sample correspondence, keeping the representations themselves unchanged and shuffling which row belongs to which text.

Value
Observed CKA (step 0, final)0.321
100 shuffles0.095 ± 0.009 (maximum 0.118)

The observed value is well above the shuffled distribution, suggesting sample correspondence beyond this chance baseline. It still doesn't mean “32% of the structure was preserved,” or that similarity rankings stayed the same.

Takeaway: these two metrics aren't interchangeable. Directions can become unaligned while some correspondence between sample relationships remains.

When do meaning and language become separable?

I used linear probes, fitting logistic regression with 5-fold cross-validation on one layer's hidden states, for two tasks:

TaskStep 0Final
9-way semantic-category classification0.3970.793
Chinese/English language classification0.8570.920

Language classification was already at 0.857 before training. Semantic-category accuracy of 0.397 was also well above the 9-class chance level of 0.111 (3.6 times higher). The initialized representations already contained signals that the classifier could use. Under the same protocol, training increased category accuracy from 39.7% to 79.3%.

This accuracy isn't the same as having “learned semantics.” Vocabulary, punctuation, length, or templates could also distinguish the categories. Comparing how quickly language and semantic signals emerge would require a separate fit.

The drift curves for the 9 categories also nearly overlap (the bottom-right panel of the figure in Section 3). Within this probe set, the drift appears to be shared across categories rather than confined to one input type.

7. Debugging notes

I found and fixed three bugs during the analysis. The third came up in a later cross-check.

Bug 1: the CKA input dimensions. My CKA function expected [checkpoints, samples, features], but I had put the 5 sampled layers on the last axis. That effectively told it each sample had 5 features. The resulting heatmap was wrong.

Bug 2: CKA normalization. I used matrix multiplication where I needed the Frobenius inner product sum(G_i ∘ G_j). The clue was the diagonal: a CKA matrix should have ones there, and mine didn't. After fixing this, I realized the unexplained dark bands were coming from the first bug.

Bug 3: printing the wrong checkpoint. My summary script used argmin|step - 100| to select the nearest checkpoint. It selected step 128 but printed “100.” The resulting statement, “82.8% of the drift completed by step 100,” was wrong in both its label and its interpretation.

These bugs made me pay more attention to two details: whether the diagonal of a similarity matrix behaves as expected, and whether the checkpoint label matches the one the script actually selected. Being able to plot a curve doesn’t mean I’ve computed or interpreted it correctly.

8. Limitations

  • The probe set contains 126 hand-written texts, rather than millions of tokens. All of these observations are limited by that coverage; I haven't established that they generalize to broader corpora.
  • I sampled only 5 / 16 layers.
  • The max-activating exemplars come from text fragments retained before the model crashed, rather than a large-scale corpus scan.
  • Pythia's learning rate never reaches 0 in these checkpoints, so they don't settle whether the representations would eventually converge. Setting the learning rate to 0 would itself stop weight updates; that's not the experiment I need. The question is whether representation dynamics remain beyond the rate associated with the learning-rate schedule. The event near 116k is worth investigating, but doesn't yet settle that question.
  • I don't know what caused the 116k event. Possible connections to data-order boundaries, precision, or optimizer state would need checks against the training configuration and full weights. I have observed coherent representation changes; their cause and behavioral implications remain open.

9. What’s next

I'd like to understand the interval in Section 4.3: why did representation displacement jump by roughly 22-fold while mean loss on the current probe set changed relatively little?

One next step is to repeat the checks at other Pythia scales (70M to 12B), looking for similar events and whether their frequency changes with scale. That would help me see whether this observation is specific to this model.

Another is to replace the hand-written probe set with a few hundred thousand tokens from the Pile. Right now, every claim about continued drift rests on those 126 texts.

Those are the two checks I'd like to try next.

Takeaways

This experiment made me more aware of how the reference point, metric, and layer shape my reading of “how much drift.” A few things I’m taking away:

  1. I find it helpful to look at distance from initialization alongside displacement between neighboring checkpoints. Using the first to answer the second can exaggerate early movement and hide later changes. The initialization-relative quantity isn't even monotonic.
  2. I had been changing two things at once. Keeping the metric fixed made the difference between reference points easier to see.
  3. Looking at loss alongside representation metrics helped. Around 116k, they did not show equally pronounced changes.
  4. I also started paying attention to how residual changes relate to the learning-rate trend. Here, looking at the residuals is what led me to the interval near 116k.
